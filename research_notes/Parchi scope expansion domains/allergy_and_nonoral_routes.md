# Allergy-scope expansion and non-oral route checks for Parchi (as of Oct 2026)

Scope note for the report writer: Parchi already has `patient.allergies?: string[]` plus draft `allergy_groups` in `public/data/alert_rules.json` (penicillin, cephalosporin, sulfonamide, nsaid, aspirin, macrolide, fluoroquinolone, tetracycline). Every group cites "Khan DA et al., Drug allergy: a 2022 practice parameter update (JACI 2022;150:1333). Recalled, not re-read". `src/engine/ledger/ledger.ts` drops non-systemic routes (`topical, ophthalmic, otic, nasal, vaginal, inhaled, rectal`) from the mg/day ledger with a `not_systemic` note, so an eye drop raises no dose or interaction conflicts today. In this session the full text of the 2022 parameter was obtained (UC Davis-hosted PDF, DOI 10.1016/j.jaci.2022.08.028) and checked with text search, so the allergy findings below are from the primary source, not from memory. Consensus-based statement (CBS) numbers 14, 17, 21, 22, 23 and 34 were confirmed in the body text. Other CBS numbers come from the order of Table III, whose PDF layout was partly scrambled, so they should be checked before they are cited in data files.

## A1. Which cross-reactivity groups should the allergy rule cover beyond the current set?

### Takeaway
The 2022 AAAAI/ACAAI parameter supports more precise beta-lactam rules (R1 side chains, carbapenem and aztreonam exemptions), a NSAID split (COX-1 cross-reactive versus single-drug, with COX-2 inhibitors as the alternative), and explicit lists of drugs that are not cross-reactive (non-antibiotic sulfonamides, including the glaucoma drugs dorzolamide, brinzolamide and acetazolamide). Each of these lets Parchi suppress false alerts as well as raise real ones. The parameter does not cover tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, ACE-inhibitor angioedema, opioids, local anaesthetics, contrast media or the aromatic anticonvulsant group, so those need other sources. The repo's tetracycline group currently cites a paper that does not support it.

### Cited Findings
**Beta-lactams (2022 parameter, primary text)**
- Cross-reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins "is largely based on R1 side chains, with identical side chains in patients with IgE-mediated allergy posing the highest risk. Rarely, cross-reactivity has been demonstrated through R2 side chains and the beta-lactam ring" (Fig 2 and Table XII) — [Khan et al., JACI 2022 full text](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Table XII groups beta-lactams with identical R1 side chains. The groups legible in the extracted text are: amoxicillin/ampicillin with cefadroxil, cefaclor, cephalexin, cefprozil, cephradine, cefatrizine and loracarbef (the aminopenicillin group); ceftriaxone with cefotaxime, cefpodoxime, cefditoren and cefepime; and ceftazidime with aztreonam. The extracted text was scrambled, so the table should be transcribed from the original PDF — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- "Patients who are proven to be aminopenicillin-allergic should generally avoid cephalosporins with identical R1-group side chains." In Europe, 12% to 38% of penicillin-allergic patients are selectively allergic to aminopenicillins — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Table III statements: after penicillin anaphylaxis, "a structurally dissimilar R1 side chain cephalosporin can be administered without testing or additional precautions". With an "unverified (not confirmed) nonanaphylactic penicillin allergy, a cephalosporin can be administered without testing or additional precautions". CBS 14, the reverse case: with an unverified nonanaphylactic cephalosporin allergy, a penicillin can be given without testing — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- The parameter says this guidance "may require an allergy alert override in electronic health records in patients with a history of penicillin allergy who are prescribed cephalosporins, although some US health systems have been able to inactivate such alerts" — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- CBS 17, carbapenems: "in patients with a history of penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, a carbapenem may be administered without testing or additional precautions". The evidence cited is a meta-analysis of 11 studies (1,127 patients) giving 0.87% cross-reactivity (95% CI 0.32%-2.32%), and a prospective study in which all 211 patients with skin-test-confirmed penicillin allergy tolerated carbapenems. The exception is a severe delayed cutaneous or organ-involved reaction — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Aztreonam may be given to penicillin- or cephalosporin-allergic patients without prior testing "unless there is a history of ceftazidime allergy", because the two share an identical R1 side chain — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- A 2024 survey of drug allergy experts restates that immediate cephalosporin cross-reactivity is "largely related to antigenic responses to R1 side chains rather than β-lactam ring cross-reactivity or R2 side chains". It reports that cefazolin's side chains are unique, and that in a 2021 study of 452 penicillin-allergic patients none had a positive skin test to cefazolin or ceftriaxone — [Brameli, Stone, Phillips, JACI Global 2024 (PMC11609518)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11609518)

**Sulfonamides**
- Table XIV, "Drugs with no or weak evidence of cross-reactivity in patients with a history of a sulfonamide antimicrobial adverse reaction", lists tamsulosin; ibutilide and sotalol; topiramate; the carbonic anhydrase inhibitors acetazolamide, methazolamide, dorzolamide and brinzolamide; celecoxib; furosemide and bumetanide; the sulfonylureas glimepiride, glyburide and gliclazide; hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, indapamide, metolazone and diazoxide; sumatriptan and naratriptan; and sulfur, sulfates and sulfites — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- The reason given: antimicrobial sulfonamides carry an aromatic amine at the N4 position and non-antimicrobials do not, "Because of this, there is minimal concern for cross-reactivity". Sulfonamides are "the second most commonly reported allergy" in health records — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Strom et al. (NEJM 2003, UK GPRD cohort): 9.9% of patients who had reacted to a sulfonamide antibiotic later reacted to a sulfonamide non-antibiotic (adjusted OR 2.8). The risk after penicillin in the same patients was higher (OR 3.9), which points to a general allergic predisposition rather than sulfonamide cross-reactivity — [themednet summary of Strom NEJM 2003;349:1628](https://www.themednet.org/publications/absence-of-cross-reactivity-between-sulfonamide-antibiotics-and-sulfonamide-nonantibiotics); [NPS MedicineWise "Sulfur allergy label is misleading"](https://www.nps.org.au/assets/cd70affdf6759bdc-a568b7738bcc-27655e9a03d1e58d4a462d700fa8855e039b96cfee3061c3e97e54afd4f7.pdf)

**Fluoroquinolones and macrolides**
- CBS 21 (conditional, low certainty): for nonanaphylactic reactions to fluoroquinolones or macrolides, use a 1- or 2-step challenge without skin testing first — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Fluoroquinolones: rashes occur in 2%-3% of courses. Cross-reactivity for delayed rashes is low (only 10% of patients with a gemifloxacin rash reacted to ciprofloxacin). IgE-mediated cross-reactivity within the class is "~50%" from limited case series. Anaphylaxis occurs at 1-5 per 100,000 prescriptions, most often with moxifloxacin. 65%-75% of patients with convincing immediate-reaction histories tolerate a rechallenge — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Macrolides: delayed rashes occur in about 1% of patients. Only about 5% of patients with convincing histories are confirmed allergic on evaluation, and "the extent of allergic cross-reactivity among macrolides is unknown" — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)

**NSAIDs and AERD**
- There are four main NSAID reaction phenotypes: AERD, NSAID-induced urticaria/angioedema, NSAID-exacerbated cutaneous disease, and single-NSAID reactions. AERD and exacerbated cutaneous disease are caused by COX-1 inhibition, are not IgE-mediated and are not drug-specific. Single-NSAID reactions "are not cross-reactive with other structurally unrelated NSAIDS" — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- CBS 22 (conditional, low certainty): "a selective COX-2 inhibitor may be used as an alternative analgesic in patients with any NSAID hypersensitivity phenotype when an NSAID is needed". Reactions to selective COX-2 inhibitors in AERD are "extremely rare" — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Weak COX-1 inhibitors (non-acetylated salicylates, acetaminophen) "may cause reactions in highly sensitive individuals if administered at higher doses (650-1000 mg) but are typically mild" — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- About 7% of adults with asthma, and one-third of those with asthma plus nasal polyps, have AERD — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Table XXI classifies NSAIDs by structure: salicylates; propionic acids (ibuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen, flurbiprofen); acetic acids (diclofenac, etodolac, indomethacin, ketorolac, sulindac); fenamates (mefenamic acid); enolic acids/oxicams (meloxicam, piroxicam); coxibs (celecoxib, parecoxib, etoricoxib); nonacidic (nabumetone). This table can be used as data for single-NSAID-reaction grouping — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)

**HLA-linked severe cutaneous reactions (South Asia relevance)**
- Table X of the parameter gives the HLA-B*15:02 frequency for carbamazepine SJS/TEN as about 4% in "South Central Asia", and 10%-15% in Han Chinese. Screening is routine in many Southeast Asian countries. HLA-B*58:01 (allopurinol SJS/TEN/DRESS) is listed as "consider use for risk stratification. Current use is not routine" — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- North Indian frequency study: 5,469 people genotyped, HLA-B*15:02 found at about 1.2% — [Mishra et al., Vacres 2022](https://vacres.pasteur.ac.ir/article-1-304-en.html)
- Indians (mostly of South Indian origin, living in Malaysia): 40% of carbamazepine SJS/TEN cases carried the allele versus 3.8% of tolerant controls. This rests on only 5 cases; the pooled OR was about 38.5 (95% CI 6.83-217.34) — [Khor et al., Epilepsia 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25266342)
- CPIC (2017): HLA-B*15:02-positive patients who have not taken carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine should not start either, unless one has been used for more than 3 months without skin reactions. For phenytoin-naive carriers, CPIC says do not use phenytoin (strong). For allopurinol, HLA-B*58:01-positive means "contraindicated" (1A, strong) — [CPIC CBZ/OXC guideline](https://cpicpgx.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/HLA_CBZ_OXC-CPIC-Guideline_7.31.2017.docx); [CPIC-derived recommendation table, PMC8024462](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8024462/table/T2); [CPIC phenytoin supplement](https://cpicpgx.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/PhenytoinManuscript_Supplement_final-draft_CPIC-review.docx)
- Testing is not feasible in many low-resource Asian settings, so clinicians sometimes avoid carbamazepine altogether — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/proof-of-concept-study-aims-for-rapid-genotyping-to-guide-epilepsy-treatment)

**Opioids**
- Opioid allergy alerts at two US systems (more than 700,000 alerts in one year): 71.8% were overridden. About three in four were for medium- or low-severity reactions. The authors recommend moving non-severe or non-immune entries to non-interruptive alerts — [Wasserman et al., BMJ Health Care Inform 2025;32:e101259](https://informatics.bmj.com/content/32/1/e101259)
- Many opioid allergy alerts fire on class cross-reactivity rather than an exact match (codeine allergy to oxycodone, for example). Non-immune "pseudoallergic" reactions come from direct histamine release by mast cells — [High Override Rate for Opioid DAI Alerts, PMC6989033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6989033/)

**Local anaesthetics**
- Ester local anaesthetics are metabolised to PABA, so an allergy to one ester is treated as an allergy to all esters. Amide allergy is "extremely rare", and reactions are often due to additives (methylparaben, which is also metabolised to PABA, and metabisulfite). True immunological reactions are a small fraction of adverse reactions to local anaesthetics. The paper-to-statement mapping comes from search summaries — [J Can Dent Assoc 2005;71(3):193](https://www.cda-adc.ca/jcda/vol-71/issue-3/193.pdf); [AAAAI Ask the Expert: lidocaine](https://www.aaaai.org/ask-the-expert/lidocaine); [Dovepress J Asthma Allergy 2021](https://www.dovepress.com/identification-of-risk-factors-and-cross-reactivity-of-local-anestheti-peer-reviewed-article-JAA)
- BSACI published "Allergy to Local and General Anaesthetics" in February 2026. It exists but was not read in this session — [BSACI 2026](https://www.bsaci.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Allergy-to-Local-and-General-Anaesthetics.pdf)

**Coverage check of the cited source (text search of the full 2022 parameter)**
- No mentions of "tetracycline", "aminoglycoside", "gentamicin", "ACE inhibitor" or "lamotrigine". Carbamazepine appears only in the HLA context. Opioids, local anaesthetics and contrast media are not sections of the 2022 update (they were covered in the 2010 parameter) — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)

- **Fix existing data (about 1 h, high value for credibility):** (1) The `tetracycline` group, and the class-wide `macrolide` grouping, cite Khan 2022, but that source has no tetracycline section and says macrolide cross-reactivity is "unknown". Re-source these groups or narrow them to exact-ingredient matches. (2) Replace "recalled, not re-read" with page-level citations: Table XII for side chains, CBS 17 for carbapenems, Table XIV for sulfonamide non-antibiotics, CBS 22 and Table XXI for NSAIDs, Table X for HLA.

- **Allergy matching must ignore the route.** The ledger's `not_systemic` skip must not be reused by the allergy rule. ## A2. Excipient and inactive-ingredient allergies and intolerances; is Indian excipient data machine-readable?

### Takeaway
Excipient allergy is very rare (CBS 34). The practical India hooks are gelatin capsules for vegetarian and religious reasons, and a "check the pack" prompt. No machine-readable excipient source for Indian brands was found. US DailyMed/openFDA SPL data is structured but US-only and about 40% inconsistent. For a hackathon, build a form-based prompt (capsule means probably gelatin), not an excipient database.

### Cited Findings
- CBS 34: "excipients are a very rare cause of immediate or delayed reactions associated with drugs". Excipient hypersensitivity may be considered after anaphylaxis to two or more structurally unrelated products that share an excipient (for example PEG, or carboxymethylcellulose in injectable corticosteroids). "Excipients are more likely to contribute to intolerance than to a true allergic reaction." The average oral formulation has about 9 inactive ingredients. The listed categories include lactose, mannitol, gelatin, cornstarch, PEG, dyes, and propylene glycol (delayed reactions) — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- India gelatin: in 2016 CDSCO sought comment on replacing gelatin capsules with cellulose capsules. At the time, 98% of the Indian pharma industry used animal-based capsules. No later binding rule was found — [Outsourcing-Pharma 2016](https://outsourcing-pharma.com/Article/2016/04/11/India-seeks-input-on-proposal-to-replace-gelatin-with-cellulose-in-capsules); [European response, Outsourcing-Pharma 2016](https://outsourcing-pharma.com/Article/2016/04/12/European-group-responds-to-Indian-proposal-to-replace-gelatin-in-capsules); [Biotecnika 2017 (industry pushback)](https://www.biotecnika.org/2017/10/drug-firms-urge-pmo-intervene-act-gelatine-capsule-replacement)
- The green/brown dot vegetarian mark is a food (FSSAI) rule. FSSAI acted against supplements with gelatin that showed a green dot. A fact-check found no Drugs and Cosmetics Act rule requiring a veg/non-veg dot on medicines — [FSSAI 2019 notice](https://fssai.gov.in/upload/media/FSSAI_News_Animal_FNB_11_10_2019.pdf); [AltNews fact-check](https://www.altnews.in/fact-check-does-himalayas-neem-tulasi-lasuna-supplements-contain-bovine-extract/)
- US structured excipient data: DailyMed SPL exposes "INACTIVE INGREDIENT" with UNII codes — [Freyr: what is DailyMed](https://www.freyrsolutions.com/what-is-dailymed). A July 2026 dataset counted structured inactive-ingredient declarations across 51,065 US oral product NDC listings, and warns that "structured presence does not establish completeness" — [Zenodo 2026 record](https://zenodo.org/records/21515694). The FDA Inactive Ingredient Database covers NDA/ANDA excipients and has had maximum-daily-exposure values since 2020 — [FDA IID](https://www.fda.gov/Drugs/InformationOnDrugs/ucm567080.htm)
- Excipient lists were reported inconsistent in about 40% of DailyMed labels (podcast discussion of a study, secondary source) — [PoTScast: drug excipients and label inconsistencies](https://the-potscast.castos.com/episodes/drug-excipients-and-label-inconsistencies-with-data-scientist-mike-brook)

### Inferences
- **Build (1-2 h):** an optional patient field `dietary_restrictions?: ('vegetarian'|'no_gelatin'|'no_alcohol'|'lactose')[]`, plus a rule that fires when the form is capsule or softgel: "Capsule shells in India are commonly gelatin (animal-derived); confirm with the pack/manufacturer if the customer needs vegetarian". Tier Silent or Inform. The source is the 98% figure above. This informs and claims nothing about the specific brand. Pitch value is high for Indian customers (Jain, vegetarian, halal), and the risk is low.
- **Do not build** excipient-level allergy matching for Indian brands. No data source exists, and using US SPL data for Indian brands would make up compositions. Instead, a free-text allergy that is not a known ingredient or group (for example "tartrazine", "lactose") should produce an Inform: "this allergy is to a possible inactive ingredient; Parchi does not have excipient data for Indian brands; check the pack".
- Months-scale option: manual curation of excipients for the top ~200 Indian brands from pack inserts or leaflets, with `source` and `pharmacist_approved` fields.

### Gaps
- No sourced findings this session on: benzyl alcohol in neonates (gasping syndrome), propylene glycol limits, ethanol in syrups, sulphites, tartrazine/azo dyes, peanut or soya oil, wheat starch/gluten, or lactose amounts in tablets. The likely best single source is the EMA "Annex to the guideline on excipients in the labelling and package leaflet" (EMA/CHMP/302620/2017), which lists thresholds and warnings per excipient, but it was not fetched.
- No CDSCO or Indian Pharmacopoeia rule on excipient disclosure on labels was found, and no Indian machine-readable excipient database was found.

## A3. Food, latex and egg allergies relevant to medicines

### Takeaway
Egg allergy is not a reason to withhold influenza vaccine; the evidence is strong (CDC ACIP, ASCIA). The propofol and latex concerns are weakly evidenced and out of scope for a retail dispensing checker. A food-allergy-to-drug map should be tiny and mostly suppress myths rather than raise alerts.

### Cited Findings
- CDC ACIP has a GRADE review on influenza vaccination in egg allergy — [CDC ACIP GRADE: influenza and egg allergy](https://cdc.gov/acip/grade/influenza-egg-allergy.html)
- Residual egg protein in flu vaccines is usually under 1 microgram per dose, far below the roughly 130 micrograms estimated to trigger a reaction when eaten. ASCIA advises that egg-allergic people can be vaccinated — [ASCIA: egg allergy and flu vaccine](https://allergy.org.au/patients/food-allergy/egg-allergy-flu-vaccine)
- In one prospective study, 457 doses of inactivated vaccine were given to 367 patients, 132 of them with severe egg allergy, and there was no anaphylaxis — [CHOP news on JACI study](https://www.chop.edu/news/journals-egg-allergic-patients-can-be-safely-vaccinated-against-influenza); [Infection Control Today](https://www.infectioncontroltoday.com/view/egg-allergy-not-reason-avoid-flu-vaccine-after-all)

### Inferences
- Include a small `food_allergy_drug_map` with Silent notes such as "egg allergy: flu vaccine can be given (ACIP/ASCIA)", so the pharmacist sees that Parchi considered it. Effort: 1 h. Pitch: medium, as a "we don't cry wolf" example.
- Most e-pharmacy carts will not contain propofol or latex-containing devices. Leave these out of scope.

### Gaps
- Propofol in egg or soy allergy: only a low-quality consumer source was found (not cited). A proper source would be anaesthesia society guidance.
- Latex in vial stoppers and packaging: not researched.
- Food-derived ingredients in Indian medicines (fish oil, lanolin or wool fat in topicals, peanut oil in some injectables): not researched.

## A4. Capturing allergy type and severity, and avoiding allergy-alert fatigue

### Takeaway
Allergy alerts are overridden 70%-90% of the time, and most overrides are appropriate because allergy lists are wrong or the alerts are too broad. The fix in the evidence is structured capture (type, criticality, verification, reaction) and tiering, so that only severe or verified matches interrupt. FHIR AllergyIntolerance provides a ready schema, which also fits Parchi's CSV/FHIR-compatibility rule.

### Cited Findings
- Across more than 150,000 allergy warnings, clinicians overrode 81% in hospital and 77% in outpatients. Reviewers judged over 96% of the overrides appropriate. The most common reason was that the patient had already tolerated the drug — [AHRQ PSNet summary of Slight et al., BMJ Qual Saf 2016](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/cross-sectional-observational-study-high-override-rates-drug-allergy-alerts-inpatient-and)
- In primary care e-prescribing, 20,430 of 26,408 drug-allergy alerts (77.4%) were overridden, most often because the drug had been tolerated before — [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1650955)
- 71.8% of opioid allergy alerts were overridden. Among the overridden alerts, 29.3% were true immune-mediated reactions. The recommendation is to make non-severe or non-immune entries non-interruptive and to record patient-specific tolerance — [Wasserman et al., BMJ HCI 2025](https://informatics.bmj.com/content/32/1/e101259); [HealthSystemCIO coverage](https://healthsystemcio.com/2025/05/29/overridden-alerts-raise-fatigue-alarm-health-systems-should-rethink-opioid-allergy-warnings-paper-finds/)
- CBS 4 (2022 parameter): "We recommend that a proactive effort should be made to delabel patients with reported penicillin allergy". The parameter also says over 95% of reported penicillin allergies are not true allergy (secondary summary) — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf); [Nebraska Medicine ASAP summary](https://asap.nebraskamed.com/2022/11/18/addressing-reported-penicillin-allergy-a-2022-practice-parameter-update/)
- FHIR R4 AllergyIntolerance fields: `type` = allergy | intolerance (allergy is "typically type I hypersensitivity, plus other allergy-like reactions, including pseudoallergy"; intolerance is "typically non-immune adverse reactions"); `category` = food | medication | environment | biologic; `criticality` = low | high | unable-to-assess ("Estimate of the potential clinical harm"); `verificationStatus` = unconfirmed | confirmed | refuted | entered-in-error; `reaction.severity` = mild | moderate | severe; `reaction.manifestation`; and `reaction.exposureRoute`. The type "is not a proxy for seriousness or risk of harm", which is better shown by manifestation and criticality — [HL7 FHIR R4 AllergyIntolerance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/allergyintolerance.html)
- Table XV of the 2022 parameter treats SJS, TEN, DRESS, AGEP, drug-induced nephritis and drug-induced hepatitis as exclusions from rechallenge. These are the "never again" severe delayed reactions — [Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Contract (additive, optional):** keep `allergies?: string[]` and add `allergy_details?: { substance: string; type?: 'allergy'|'intolerance'; reaction?: 'anaphylaxis'|'angioedema'|'urticaria'|'rash'|'sjs_ten_dress'|'gi_upset'|'other'; criticality?: 'low'|'high'|'unable-to-assess'; verified?: 'unconfirmed'|'confirmed'|'refuted'; tolerated?: string[] }[]`. These map one-to-one to FHIR. Effort: 2-3 h including the UI chips.
- **Tier policy (deterministic):**
  - Interrupt: an exact ingredient match where criticality is high or unknown, or any match where the reaction is anaphylaxis or SJS/TEN/DRESS.
  - Inform: a cross-reactive group match (for example an identical R1 side chain).
  - Silent: an intolerance (for example "GI upset to ibuprofen" gives no alert, only a note); a drug the patient has tolerated before; and the explicit no-cross-reactivity lists (Table XIV, carbapenems, coxibs).
  - Pitch: "we follow the 2022 practice parameter to not alert", which differs from the competitors' 77%-90% override rates.
- **Risk:** customers' self-reported allergy labels in Indian e-pharmacy flows are probably free text and vague ("sulfa", "antibiotic", "painkiller"). Parchi should map vague labels to Inform with a request to clarify, never to Interrupt on a whole class.

### Gaps
- No India-specific data was found on allergy-label accuracy or alert override rates in Indian pharmacy or hospital systems.

## B1. Eye-drop (ophthalmic) rules with systemic or local risk

### Takeaway
Several eye drops have well-documented systemic effects that labels list as contraindications: timolol in asthma, COPD and bradycardia; timolol with verapamil; brimonidine under 2 years; phenylephrine 10% in hypertension or under 1 (US) or under 12 (UK) years; cyclopentolate and atropine in infants. These can be built as a small curated `route_rules` table keyed on ingredient + route (+ strength/age), using drug-as-diagnosis proxies when conditions are not captured. Steroid eye-drop misuse is the strongest India-specific harm story.

### Cited Findings
**Beta-blocker eye drops (timolol and others)**
- About 80% of topically applied timolol is absorbed through the conjunctival and nasal mucosa, bypassing first-pass metabolism. Pressing on the tear duct reduces absorption — [Cyprus J Med Sci 2021 case report](https://cyprusjmedsci.com/articles/an-unexpected-cause-of-symptomatic-bradycardia-anti-glaucoma-eye-drops/cjms.2021.2546)
- US label contraindications: bronchial asthma or a history of it, severe COPD, sinus bradycardia, second- or third-degree AV block, overt cardiac failure, cardiogenic shock — [Apotex timolol ophthalmic US label](https://www.apotex.com/products/us/downloads/pre/timo_opso_oad.pdf); [drugs.com timolol ophthalmic disease interactions](https://www.drugs.com/disease-interactions/timolol-ophthalmic,timoptic.html)
- Irish SmPC: "Caution should be used in the co-administration of beta-adrenergic agents, such as Timolol and oral or intravenous calcium antagonists, because of possible atrioventricular conduction disturbances, left ventricular failure and hypotension. In patients with impaired cardiac function, co-administration should be avoided." Combining with a systemic beta-blocker can cause excessive beta-blockade, and two topical beta-blockers are not recommended — [HPRA timolol SmPC](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/16649/LicenseSPC_PA0290-072-001_01072008020048.pdf); [iatroX timolol summary](https://www.iatrox.com/medicines/timolol-maleate/clinical)
- A 1987 BMJ report described severe bradycardia from timolol eye drops with verapamil. A 2021 case report of symptomatic bradycardia from timolol-brinzolamide concludes that drug histories should include eye drops — [iatroX](https://www.iatrox.com/medicines/timolol-maleate/clinical); [Cyprus J Med Sci 2021](https://cyprusjmedsci.com/articles/an-unexpected-cause-of-symptomatic-bradycardia-anti-glaucoma-eye-drops/cjms.2021.2546)

**Paediatric and systemic toxicity**
- Brimonidine is contraindicated in neonates and infants under 2 years (US PI). Post-marketing reports include apnoea, bradycardia, coma, hypotension, hypothermia, hypotonia and respiratory depression. This applies to all strengths and to the timolol combination — [Drugs.com brimonidine PI](https://Drugs.com/pro/brimonidine-eye-drops.html); [PrescriberPoint brimonidine](https://prescriberpoint.com/therapies/brimonidine-tartrate-383421a); [brimonidine-timolol](https://prescriberpoint.com/therapies/brimonidine-tartrate-timolol-maleate-ophthalmic-solution-533f997)
- Phenylephrine 10% ophthalmic "is contraindicated in patients with hypertension, or thyrotoxicosis"; 2.5% should be used instead. It is also contraindicated under age 1. Serious, sometimes fatal, cardiovascular events (ventricular arrhythmia, MI) have occurred, mainly in patients with cardiovascular disease — [FDA label 203510s005](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2022/203510s005lbl.pdf). UK SmPC: 10% is contraindicated in children under 12 — [emc phenylephrine 10% SmPC](https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/medicine/17619/spc)
- Cyclopentolate can cause CNS toxicity (ataxia, hallucinations, seizures, psychosis), especially in infants and young children. Labels advise a single 1% drop, nasolacrimal pressure, and observing infants for 30 minutes. A South African Minims label says not under 1 year — [MedLibrary cyclopentolate](https://medlibrary.org/lib/rx/meds/cyclopentolate-hydrochloride/); [Health Canada PM](https://pdf.hres.ca/dpd_pm/00058057.PDF); [SAHPRA PI](https://pi-pil-repository.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/PI-20230328.pdf)
- Atropine eye drops: systemic toxicity (ataxia, restlessness, hallucinations) is reported especially in infants and the elderly. Infants and children with Down syndrome, spastic paralysis or brain damage are more susceptible — [Medsafe atropine datasheet](https://www.medsafe.govt.nz/Profs/Datasheet/a/Atropteyedrop.pdf). Low-dose atropine 0.01% paediatric drops are sold in India (for example "Atroped 0.01 Paediatric") — [Apollo Pharmacy listing](https://www.apollopharmacy.in/medicine/atroped-0-01-paediatric-eye-drops-5-ml)

**Steroid eye drops**
- In rural India, children with vernal keratoconjunctivitis misused topical steroids. The authors recommend weaker steroids and fortnightly IOP and vision monitoring for children on topical steroids — [Indian J Ophthalmol 2019 (PubMed 31546501)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31546501/). AAO EyeNet commentary: two-thirds of the affected children were blind in one or both eyes, and blame lies more with prescribers than with parents using OTC drops — [AAO EyeNet "Steroid overuse in India"](https://aao.org/eyenet/article/steroid-overuse-in-india)
- In a Karnataka survey of 89 pharmacy workers (2010), steroids were among the eye drops most often dispensed without a prescription, and only about 40% knew the complications of steroids — [Cogprints 7010](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/7010/)
- Delhi ophthalmologists warned against irrational steroid eye-drop use during the 2023 conjunctivitis surge. Children's IOP responds faster to topical steroids — [OneIndia 2023](https://www.oneindia.com/india/amid-conjunctivitis-eye-flu-surge-doctors-warn-against-irrational-use-of-steroid-eye-drops-3602323.html). A cited Indian study reported that 99.1% of people did not know the difference between OTC and prescription eye drops. Posterior subcapsular cataract is a known steroid complication — [Cureus 2023 (PMC10484037)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10484037)
- Chloramphenicol + dexamethasone eye drops are sold in India (for example "Chlorocol Plus"). The listing shows it as Rx-only — [Apollo Pharmacy listing](https://qapharmacyfe.apollopharmacy.in/otc/chlorocol-plus-eye-drops-5ml)

**Sulfonamide CAIs and preservatives**
- Dorzolamide, brinzolamide and acetazolamide are on the 2022 parameter's no/weak cross-reactivity list for sulfonamide-antibiotic allergy — [Khan et al., JACI 2022 Table XIV](https://health.ucdavis.edu/media-resources/antibiotic-stewardship/documents/pdfs/guidelines/drug-allergy-aaaai-2022.pdf)
- Benzalkonium chloride-preserved drops: remove soft contact lenses before use and do not reinsert for at least 15 minutes, because BAK can be absorbed into lenses and discolour them. Some recent studies question whether the wait is necessary — [emc SmPC example](https://emc.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/13181/smpc); [Review of Contact Lenses "BAKtracking"](https://www.reviewofcontactlenses.com/article/baktracking-contact-lens-guidelines)

### Inferences
Proposed `route_rules` table (new file `public/data/route_rules.json`). Each row has `id, ingredients|classes, route, condition (age / co-medication proxy / strength / duration), tier, message, source, pharmacist_approved:false`. The engine evaluates it on all resolved lines regardless of the ledger.

## B2. Ear, nasal, inhaled and topical (skin) rules

### Takeaway
The strongest buildable rules are: nasal decongestant longer than 5 days (MHRA, April 2026); aminoglycoside ear drops where the eardrum may not be intact (ask, do not assume); total corticosteroid load across inhaled, nasal and oral routes; SABA-only reliever (GINA 2024); and Indian steroid-antifungal(-antibiotic) cream FDCs, which are tied to the dermatophytosis epidemic. Most need a duration from the sig (already parsed: "x5d") or a "ask the customer" Inform rather than knowledge of a diagnosis.

### Cited Findings
**Otic**
- The Australian otolaryngology society position statement recommends avoiding ototoxic ear drops in perforated eardrums or patent grommets where possible. The incidence of hearing or balance loss from aminoglycoside drops is about 1 in 10,000, and the true rate is unknown — [MJA 2007;186(11) position statement](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2007/186/11/ototoxic-ear-drops-grommet-and-tympanic-membrane-perforations-position); [NPS Australian Prescriber](https://www.nps.org.au/assets/693071246ac27876-e9ad2e300428-92be680768f053600490981a9c8aaedc551dcf36c704dea7eff68e099535.pdf)
- AAO-HNS bulletin: ototoxic drops "should NOT be utilized when the tympanic membrane is perforated and the middle ear space is open" when non-ototoxic drugs work equally well — [ENTnet Bulletin](https://bulletin.entnet.org/home/article/21246052/acute-otitis-externa-danger-of-using-ototoxic-topical-drops)
- Ofloxacin otic is approved for otitis media with a perforated or ventilated eardrum. Package inserts for aminoglycoside drops use softer "care" wording, and toxicity mostly follows courses longer than 14 days or repeated courses — [Nevada Medicaid otic antibiotic review](https://www.medicaid.nv.gov/Downloads/provider/NVRx_DCR_20100624_Otic_Antibiotics.pdf); [AAFP 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1015/p1870.html)

**Nasal**
- MHRA Drug Safety Update (April 2026): xylometazoline/oxymetazoline sprays and drops carry an increased risk of rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa) and tachyphylaxis with overuse. Do not use for more than 5 consecutive days, and consider other treatment if symptoms persist. Most patients recover within 3 months — [MHRA DSU 2026](https://gov.uk/drug-safety-update/nasal-decongestant-sprays-and-drops-containing-xylometazoline-hydrochloride-slash-oxymetazoline-hydrochloride-increased-risk-of-rebound-congestion-rhinitis-medicamentosa-and-tachyphylaxis-with-overuse). Saudi FDA issued a similar safety review in September 2026 — [SFDA 2026](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2026-09/RISKOF~1.PDF)
- In healthy volunteers, rebound swelling appeared after 30 days of oxymetazoline but not after 10 days, and the authors advise against more than 10 days — [study record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/1287895). This conflicts with the regulators' 5 days; use the regulator figure.

**Inhaled and corticosteroid load**
- GINA 2024: adults, adolescents and children aged 6-11 should not be treated with a SABA reliever alone. ICS-containing treatment reduces severe exacerbations. The preferred Track 1 uses as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol, which reduced severe exacerbations by about 60% compared with a SABA reliever — [asthma-edu.org.tw GINA 2024 summary](https://asthma-edu.org.tw/news-detail.jsp?pk=29); [Mo Med Sep/Oct 2024](https://digitaleditions.walsworth.com/article/Science+of+Medicine/4871786/833555/article.html); [Practice Nurse GINA 2023](https://practicenurse.co.uk/guidelines/gina-2023-guideline-in-a-nutshell)
- Inhaled steroids at 400 micrograms/day or more of beclometasone equivalent "may be associated with systemic side effects" (in children; local NHS bulletin) — [Tayside Prescriber 107, 2009](https://www.nhstaysideadtc.scot.nhs.uk/approved/bulletin/taypres/2009/Tayside%20Prescriber%20Issue%20107%20August%202009.pdf). A spacer is recommended for all patients on high-dose ICS by MDI — [NHS Highland formulary](https://rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/tam-treatments-and-medicines-nhs-highland/formularies/highland-formulary/respiratory-system/corticosteroids)

**Topical (skin) and India FDC context**
- Superficial dermatophytosis in India has been called the "Great Indian Epidemic", linked to indiscriminate OTC use of steroid-antifungal-antibiotic creams. In a rural North Indian clinic, 213 of 723 patients (29.2%) had misused topical steroids. Clobetasol propionate made up 58.2% of the misuse, pharmacists and local healers were 78% of the sources, and 80% of tinea patients had tinea incognito. The figures come from search summaries of these papers, and the exact mapping of figures to papers needs checking against the full text — [PMC8216118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8216118); [PMC8641741](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8641741); [PMC7239529](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7239529); [BMJ Open 2020;10:e032829](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/10/5/e032829); [IJDVL](https://ijdvl.com/?p=4308)
- India's September 2018 ban covered 328 FDCs, 24 of them topical-steroid combinations. A pharmacovigilance analysis found the ban reached only 3 preparations, which accounted for 50.10% of adverse events, so steroid combinations stayed widely available OTC (same source cluster as above) — [PMC8216118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8216118); [PMC7239529](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7239529)
- Trichophyton indotineae, the drug-resistant dermatophyte linked to this context, is covered in Indian press — [South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/trichophyton-indotineae-fungus-india/)
- In August 2024 the health ministry banned 156 more FDCs, including drugs for colds, fevers, bacterial and fungal infections — [Adv Pharm Bull letter, PMC11970505](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11970505/). After the 2018 ban, sales of the banned antimicrobial FDCs fell 75%, but sales of same-class substitute combinations rose — [Sulis et al., J Pharm Policy Pract 2022 (PMC9047325)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9047325/)

## B3. Which checks already exist in DDInter or similar sources, and which need a curated table? Machine-readable sources and how to plug into the pipeline

### Takeaway
DDInter 2.0 is keyed by drug, not by route or formulation, and nothing in its paper or interface mentions route filtering. Feeding non-oral lines into it naively would both catch real pairs (timolol + verapamil) and create false ones (ciprofloxacin eye drops + theophylline). The safe design is a curated route-relevance layer: a small, sourced table saying which non-oral ingredient/route pairs carry systemic interaction risk, which gates DDInter, plus a separate `route_rules` table for route-specific rules that DDInter lacks (age, duration, eardrum, FDC misuse).

### Cited Findings
- DDInter 2.0 (Nucleic Acids Research 2025, database issue): 2,310 drugs and 302,516 DDI records, plus drug-food, drug-disease and therapeutic-duplication records. Records have mechanism, risk level, management and ATC codes, and the interface filters by drug type and therapeutic area. The site states 2,290 approved drugs, which does not match the paper — [NAR 2025;53(D1):D1356](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/53/D1/D1356/7740584); [PMC11701621](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701621/); [DDInter 2.0 site](https://ddinter2.scbdd.com/)
- US SPL (DailyMed) is structured, with a route of administration and inactive ingredients with UNII codes per product, so it can act as a reference for route and excipients (US products only) — [Freyr: what is DailyMed](https://www.freyrsolutions.com/what-is-dailymed); [Zenodo 2026 SPL census](https://zenodo.org/records/21515694)
- FHIR AllergyIntolerance has `reaction.exposureRoute`, so route-aware allergy records are standard-compatible — [HL7 FHIR R4](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/allergyintolerance.html)

### Inferences
- **Recommended build order** (hackathon hours, highest pitch-per-hour first):
  1. Make the allergy rule route-agnostic, and add the sulfonamide non-antibiotic and coxib Silent lists (≈2 h).
  2. Add a `route_rules.json` engine pass with the 4-5 star rules: ophthalmic beta-blocker + oral beta-blocker/asthma proxy, nasal decongestant > 5 days, steroid-antifungal cream FDC, steroid eye drop review, brimonidine < 2 y (≈6-8 h total).
  3. Add structured allergy details (FHIR-shaped) with the tier policy (≈3 h).
  4. Add the R1 side-chain table (≈2-3 h).
  5. Add corticosteroid total load across routes (≈4-8 h, blocked on an equivalence table needing pharmacist approval).
- **Gate DDInter by route:** add `systemic_relevance` per ingredient+route (for example `timolol/ophthalmic: true`, `brimonidine/ophthalmic: true (paediatric)`, `phenylephrine/ophthalmic: true if strength ≥ 10%`, `ciprofloxacin/ophthalmic: false`, `mometasone/nasal: low`). Only pairs where both sides are relevant reach DDInter matching. Each row needs `source` and `pharmacist_approved`. Effort: 2-4 h for about 20 rows.
- **Months-scale work:** a full BNF-style route-specific interaction set would need licensed data. BNF and Medicines Complete are licensed, and no open API was found or checked. Indian route-specific guidance is not machine-readable.
- **Contract additions (additive, optional):** `ResolvedBrand.strength_percent?` (for phenylephrine 10% vs 2.5%, atropine 1% vs 0.01%); `Sig.duration_days?` (likely already parsed); `Patient.age_years` (needed for paediatric rules); and `Patient.flags?: ('contact_lenses'|'perforated_eardrum'|'asthma'|'copd')[]` as optional pharmacist-ticked context, which avoids the need for diagnoses.

### Gaps
- Whether DDInter 2.0 contains timolol(ophthalmic)-verapamil or similar pairs, and whether its drug-disease records include "timolol-asthma", was not checked directly in the database. The team should query ddinter2.scbdd.com for timolol, brimonidine and phenylephrine before building.
- BNF/NICE API licensing for Indian use was not researched.
- No Indian (CDSCO, NFI) machine-readable label repository with route or excipient fields was found.

## B4. Evidence of harm in India from non-oral medicine misuse (summary for the pitch)

### Takeaway
India has documented, citable harm from OTC topical steroid misuse in both skin and eyes (dermatophytosis epidemic; children blinded by steroid eye drops), from pharmacists dispensing steroid eye drops without prescriptions, and from incomplete FDC bans. That makes non-oral checks a strong "built for India" differentiator, more than generic DDI checking.

### Cited Findings
- Pharmacists and local healers were the main sources (78%) of topical steroid misuse in a rural North Indian study, and clobetasol was the most misused (58.2%) — [BMJ Open 2020;10:e032829 / Indian topical steroid misuse literature cluster](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/10/5/e032829); [PMC8216118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8216118)
- Steroids were among the eye drops most often dispensed without a prescription in Karnataka pharmacies, and only about 40% of pharmacy workers knew their complications — [Cogprints 7010](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/7010/)
- Two-thirds of children with steroid-induced glaucoma in an Indian VKC series were blind in one or both eyes — [AAO EyeNet](https://aao.org/eyenet/article/steroid-overuse-in-india); [IJO 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31546501/)
- The FDC bans (2018: 328 including 24 topical steroid combinations; 2024: 156) were partly undermined by same-class substitutes and remaining OTC availability — [PMC9047325](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9047325/); [PMC11970505](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11970505/); [PMC7239529](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7239529)

### Inferences
- Pitch line: "Parchi's checks reach the pharmacist at the exact moment the Indian evidence says the harm happens: the pharmacist handing over a steroid cream or steroid eye drop." The verifying-pharmacist user is the one these studies identify as the source of supply, so the alerts target the documented point of failure.
- Keep alert wording informational ("confirm diagnosis and duration"). Several of these products are legitimately prescribed, and the CLAUDE.md rule says output informs and never decides.

### Gaps
- No recent (2024-2026) national-scale data was found on OTC steroid eye-drop use or nasal decongestant overuse in India. The figures above come mostly from 2010-2021 regional studies.
- Current (2026) legal status of specific steroid-antifungal and steroid-antibiotic ophthalmic FDCs: not confirmed. Check the CDSCO banned-FDC gazette lists against Track A's brand data.
